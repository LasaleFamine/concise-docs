# Concise documentation

Help readers find the correct information and act with the necessary context. Apply this guidance to prose; preserve the syntax and semantics of code, configuration and structured records.

## Choose scope and sources

Follow repository instructions, mandatory reads and the requested format. A writing request authorizes the requested document; an assessment authorizes findings and proposals. Revise existing material when the request includes editing, within its scope. Keep local locks, ownership rules and intervening user edits intact.

Identify the reader, their task and the intended result. Check for an existing authoritative home before creating a file. Inspect entry instructions, headings and references first, then read the relevant sources. Links do not load their targets into context. Follow links needed for the task without reading the whole graph. State assumptions that affect the scope or sources.

## Write or update documentation

- Choose a structure that fits the reader's task. Lead with the purpose or result. Include the context, prerequisites, steps and expected outcome that the task needs; avoid imposing a full template on a small note.
- Give each current rule or fact one authoritative home. Update that source and link to it where readers look for it. Use direct, descriptive links to relevant sections and say when to read them. Avoid chains of pages that only point elsewhere.
- Keep constraints beside the step where a reader must act on them. A brief used offline or outside the repository needs the essential rules and facts in place. Name their source so the brief does not become a competing authority.
- Use short, active sentences, simple verbs, consistent terms and factual headings. Give each procedural step one action and make the actor clear. Define necessary terms. Preserve exact identifiers, units, qualifications, quotations and the requested language. This is STE-inspired clarity; claim neither formal ASD-STE100 compliance nor a percentage score.
- Separate verified facts, assumptions, estimates, proposals and decisions. Leave unknowns explicit. Support commands and examples with current sources; do not invent details to make a document appear complete.

## Review and simplify existing material

Choose representative reader tasks and trace how a reader reaches the required rules, facts and commands. Search for terms and read relevant sections. Classify overlapping material by role: current rules or state, procedure, evidence or history, and generated view.

Identify the authoritative source and flag conflicts. Check the source before relying on a summary. Resolve authority before removing a competing statement. Replace unnecessary repetition with direct links while retaining essential standalone context.

Update summaries that describe current state and regenerate derived views from their sources. Preserve unique evidence, dates, approval scope and historical decisions. Shorter text is useful only if the reader still gets the correct meaning and required constraints.

## Verify and report

Check new or changed links and anchors, terminology, factual claims and examples. Verify commands against current sources and use safe, relevant checks where feasible. For new documentation, walk through the intended reader task. For revisions, repeat the sampled tasks. Confirm that the reader can find the correct source, required constraints, facts and commands; state unverified steps or assumptions.

When a before-and-after comparison is needed, use the same tasks and scope. Count unique content loaded per task in words, bytes or tokens from a named tokenizer. Report the unit, link hops and whether retrieval was correct. Separate total document size from task reading cost. Label estimates and avoid arbitrary budgets or unsupported token savings.

Report the result, checks and remaining gaps briefly. Scale verification to the change; a small writing task does not require a corpus-wide audit or a reading-cost benchmark.
